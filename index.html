Buried by Vesuvius · Revealed by AI

You're looking at a book from 79 AD.

When Mount Vesuvius erupted, it buried a library of papyrus scrolls and baked them into charcoal. They're too fragile to unroll. Now X-rays and AI can read the ink hidden inside, and you can explore it right here.

The story in three steps

I

Buried in 79 AD

Vesuvius erupted and buried the town of Herculaneum, including a grand villa with a library. The heat turned its scrolls into lumps of charcoal, but also kept them safe underground for 1,700 years.

II

Too fragile to open

Found in the 1750s, most scrolls crumble if you try to unroll them. So scientists scan them with powerful X-rays and unroll them virtually, one layer at a time.

III

AI finds the ink

The ink is soot on burnt papyrus, almost invisible. An AI trained on known letters spots it. I made that step work on an ordinary laptop, so anyone can try.

What does the gold mean?

Gold is where the AI thinks there's ink. Brighter means more confident. It's an excellent guess, but not a final reading: some bright marks are cracks or fibres. Turning marks into words is careful work done by experts in ancient writing.

What you can do here

Zoom all the way in

Every piece at full size, down to single strokes, with nothing to install and no account. Works with pinch-zoom on your phone.

See the ink on the papyrus

The gold highlight sits right on top of the real X-ray scan, so you can see where the ink lies on the burnt sheet. Sliders help tired eyes.

Share the exact spot

Found something intriguing? “Share this view” sends a friend to the very place and zoom you're looking at.

And if you have a Windows PC, the free one-click app lets you read other pieces yourself, no graphics card needed.

What scholars have already read

The pictures above are the AI's view, not readings. Here is what experts in ancient writing have published so far from other scrolls (in their words, on the Vesuvius Challenge site):

Scroll 1 (PHerc. Paris. 4)

The 2023 Grand Prize: the first passages ever read from inside a closed scroll, Greek prose attributed to the philosopher Philodemus, musing on pleasure.

Scroll 4 (PHerc. 1667)

The first Herculaneum scroll to be completely unrolled and read without touching it: a philosophical work on ethics.
